Daughter of Two Worlds, Book One of The Yara Chronicles. Yara is seventeen, half human and half Vey'kar. She is learning to live on Earth while her mother Oya secretly builds Kijani, a hidden Vey'kar village deep in the jungle. Yara does not fully belong in either world, and she is still searching for the truth about her missing father, Jonah Noah Moses, whom Oya called Ayo. Did he leave, was he taken, or is he dead? The people of Kijani

The cast

Eight characters carry Book 1: the family at the center, the village around Yara, and the two humans who reach the jungle from opposite sides. Inside the village, Zola pulls Yara toward loyalty and mischief, Amara teaches her to hold both sides of herself, and Jabari tests whether a half-human princess can lead at all. Outside it, Dane Mercer is the danger of the human world finding the village — and Elena Ruiz is the proof that not every human is a threat.

Yara

The princess · 17 · Half-human, half-Vey'kar

Long dark braids with gold bands, warm brown skin, and faint glowing markings that show more when she is emotional or using her senses. Brave but not reckless — she thinks before she fights. Curious about humans, even when she's supposed to be scared of them. She hates being treated like a princess and wants to earn respect, not inherit it.

Wants most: to belong somewhere — and to find out what really happened to her father. Fears: that he left because of her, and that if humans find the village she'll have to choose between them and her mother.

Oya

The mother · Queen & founder of Kijani · Vey'kar

Taller and broader than Yara, built like a warrior-queen, with heavier braids, more gold, and markings earned over a lifetime. Strong, stubborn, and proud; protective to a fault. She shows love through action, not words, and never asks anyone to do something she wouldn't do herself. She keeps one thing of Ayo's — a carved bone charm — and won't talk about him.

Wants most: a safe home where her people don't have to hide who they are. Fears: losing Yara the way she lost her father — and failing Kijani as both leader and mother.

Jonah Noah Moses — “Ayo”

The missing father · Human · Missing about 10 years

A kind, curious explorer and researcher — the kind of human who goes looking for things in the jungle others are afraid to find, which is how he met Oya. He wasn't afraid of her; he talked to her like a person. He taught young Yara human songs and stories, and told her being half-human was a gift, not a weakness. Then he went out one day and never came back. No body. No note. No goodbye.

The mystery: did he leave, was he taken, or is he dead? Book 1 does not answer this — on purpose.

Zola

The best friend · 17 · Full Vey'kar · Tracker

Shorter and stockier than Yara, built for speed, with messier braids, bone beads instead of gold, and markings she never hides. Loud, funny, and loyal to a fault — first to laugh, and first to fight for Yara. The best tracker of their age group. Reckless, honest, and the sister Yara chose.

Wants most: to be a great hunter, prove herself to Oya, and keep Yara safe. Fears: losing Yara — to the human world, or to a trial she isn't ready for.

Jabari

The rival elder · Late 60s · Full Vey'kar

Big, broad, and scarred, with graying braids tied in iron, not gold — gold is for show, iron is for war. He taught Oya to hunt; now he's her biggest rival. Proud, stubborn, and traditional: the old hunter ways are the only ways. He is not a villain — he genuinely believes challenging Oya protects the Vey'kar. When he speaks, the whole village listens.

Wants most: a return to the old ways — no hiding, no humans, no half-human heir. His mistake: underestimating Yara.

Amara

The mentor & healer · Early 50s · Full Vey'kar

Kijani's healer, working from a hut by the river among drying herbs. Calm, patient, and wise — she listens more than she talks, and she's the only person who will tell Oya she's wrong and be heard. She understands Yara's half-human body, teaches her to dim her markings and control her senses — and she knew Ayo, liked him, and knows more about his disappearance than she lets on.

Wants most: for Yara to be whole — at peace with both sides of herself, never forced to pick one.

Dane Mercer

The threat · Early 40s · Human trophy hunter

Tall, lean, and weathered, with a scar through his left eyebrow and a notebook full of tracks, sketches, and GPS points. The best human tracker Yara and Oya have ever faced — he found the village's outer trails in weeks. He doesn't believe in monsters; he believes in money: a new species, a trophy, a photo that makes him famous. Patient enough to sit in a blind for twelve hours. Arrogant enough to think nothing can hunt him.

Wants most: proof he can sell to the world. His blind spot: he thinks the Vey'kar are animals. They are a people — with a queen, a princess, and a village.

Elena Ruiz

The ally · Late 20s · Wildlife researcher & ranger

Dark curly hair in a ponytail with a pencil stuck in it, a faded researcher vest with too many pockets, a notebook, a camera for animals rather than trophies, and a first-aid kit. Kind, curious, and brave in a human way. She believes in protecting the jungle, not exploiting it — she lost a research partner to poachers years ago, which is why she hates people like Dane. She meets Yara by accident and earns her trust slowly: Yara's first real human friend.

Wants most: to protect the jungle and everything in it — including people she didn't know existed.

The world

The Vey'kar & Kijani

The Vey'kar are a proud hunter people from another world — not monsters, but a culture, with laws, families, and honor. They came from a jungle world, hotter and wilder than Earth, with bigger predators and taller trees — which is why Earth feels almost easy to them, and why they chose the jungle for Kijani: it feels like home.

The Vey'kar

  • Appearance: tall and strong — most adults stand 6′5″ to 7′ — with warm brown to deep bronze skin and glowing markings, different for everyone, that record clan, hunts, and rank. Long braids bound with bone, gold, or iron; every hunt and trial adds a band. Amber-gold eyes with night vision.
  • Senses: hearing, smell, and tracking that put humans to shame — a heartbeat heard across a clearing, rain smelled hours before it arrives. Their markings tingle when danger is near. They are fast and strong, but not invincible: they tire, they bleed, they can die. A hunt has to be fair.
  • Craft: bone-and-gold spears, blades, and bows they forge themselves. Light-weave cloaks that bend light a little — not invisible, just blurred, like heat haze. Carved bone charms that hum when prey is near. They have ships — that's how they reached Earth — but the ships stay hidden, and Kijani lives simply, on purpose.
  • Language: deep, clicking, and musical. Yara speaks both Vey'kar and English — one more reason she belongs fully to neither world. Ayo learned it slowly, and was terrible at the clicks at first. Yara still laughs about that.
  • Yara's difference: half-human means fainter markings and senses a step below a full Vey'kar's — but a gift no full Vey'kar has: she understands humans, thinks like them, and can move in their world. Jabari calls it a weakness.

Kijani — “the green place”

  • At a glance: founded by Oya deep in the jungle, far from any human town or road, built around an old stone temple under thick canopy. Small at first — about twenty people, just a few families. Everyone knows everyone. A home being made, shelter by shelter.
  • How it stays hidden: the canopy hides it from planes and drones. There are no roads in — only hidden trails, changed often and erased after every passing. Homes blend into trees and temple stone. Torches burn only in the central clearing, shielded, so no light leaves the place and no smoke rises straight. The river gives water — and masks sound and scent.
  • The layout: the Temple at the center, where Oya holds gatherings and trials happen. Oya's hut close by — bigger than the others, but plain; she refuses to live apart from her people. Amara's healing hut by the river. The hunters' shelters up in the trees. The training ground on the edge of the village, where Jabari watches.
  • Daily life: mornings for hunting, gathering, and training. Afternoons for building — Kijani is still growing. Evenings around the fire in the temple clearing, for stories, food, and Oya settling disputes. That rhythm is what Yara is learning to protect.

The six laws of the honor code

  1. Never hunt the weakNo children, no sick, no unarmed. A hunt has to be a challenge, or it does not count.
  2. Never hunt for fun aloneEvery hunt has a purpose — food, protection, or a trial to prove yourself.
  3. Respect your preyAfter a hunt, they thank the animal and honor it. Wasting a kill is shameful.
  4. A trial makes a hunterYoung Vey'kar must pass a trial to be called a hunter. Yara's trial is coming — and Jabari wants it hard.
  5. Family and clan come firstA Vey'kar will die for their clan. Kijani is not just a village — it is a clan reborn.
  6. Keep the secretOn Earth, they must stay hidden. Revealing themselves to humans puts everyone at risk.

Why did the Vey'kar leave their homeworld?

Nobody in Kijani tells that story — not yet. Whether the homeworld was dying, at war, or something else entirely is an open thread, kept as a mystery for later in the series, just like Ayo's disappearance.

Where the book is going

The outline

Yara is a princess by birth who has not yet earned her place. To be accepted in Kijani she must pass a hunter's trial, keep the village secret from humans, and decide what to do when a human hunter finds their trail — all while the questions about her father go unanswered. Hunter law says to drive the threat away; Yara's human side says there has to be another way. By Act 3, she has to make both sides work together.

Act 1 — Arrival in Kijani

Chapters 1–5

  • Oya and Yara settle in Kijani while the village is still being built — just a few families, a temple, and a lot of work.
  • Yara learns the village rules: stay hidden, hide her markings and eyes, don't trust humans.
  • Jabari tests Yara early and finds her wanting — too human, too soft.
  • Yara finds one thing of Ayo's that Oya kept, and starts asking questions Oya won't answer.

Act 2 — Two Worlds Collide

Chapters 6–14

  • The village grows. Old hunting traditions clash with Yara's human compassion.
  • Yara's trial is announced — she must prove herself a hunter to be accepted as princess and heir. Jabari wants it hard. Maybe too hard.
  • Dane Mercer finds the first traces of Kijani and starts hunting the village.
  • Yara meets Elena Ruiz by accident, and slowly earns a first real human friend.
  • Amara hints she knows more about Ayo than she's saying.

Act 3 — The Secret Is Threatened

Chapters 15–20

  • Dane gets too close, and the village must decide how to drive him off.
  • Oya wants him gone by hunter law. Yara wants it done without killing him.
  • Jabari uses the crisis to challenge Oya publicly.
  • Climax: Yara's trial and Dane's attack collide. Yara uses both sides of herself to save Kijani without breaking the honor code.
  • Kijani is safe — for now. Dane is driven off, not dead. And Yara finds a new clue about her father.

Threads left open

What really happened to Jonah / Ayo — did he leave, was he taken, or is he alive? · Why did the Vey'kar really leave their homeworld? · And kept aside for a later book: Yara meets a human boy, and goes to a human school.

The jungle went quiet in the wrong place.

Yara stopped with one foot still raised. She had learned that quiet the hard way. The jungle was never truly silent — there were frogs, and dripping leaves, and small things moving in the dark where small things belonged. But this quiet was a hole in the sound, the shape of something big holding still inside it, the way the boar had held still on her first morning, being a tree so hard that it gave itself away.

She set her foot down without a sound, the way Zola had taught her, and breathed the way Amara had taught her. Slow. Low. Let the markings settle. The faint blue lines on her arms, already brightening with her heartbeat, dimmed again under her skin. Out here, past the last covered trail, light was a mistake. Oya had said it on the temple steps, and she said it every day after: no light leaves this place.

Then she listened with everything she had.

Water, to her left, over stone. A frog, far off, thinking better of it. Her own blood, too loud. And under it all, so faint she nearly missed it, a smell that did not belong to the green place at all.

Oil. Cleaned metal. Leather that had been treated in a factory, not cured in smoke. And human sweat — not Vey'kar sweat, which ran sharp and green like cut leaves, but the other kind. The kind she half was.

Her hand found the knife at her belt by itself. Her fingers tightened on the hilt, then eased again, and her markings dimmed as she breathed out, slow.

Her lips barely moved. “Not an animal,” she whispered, to no one. She swallowed, angry at the fear in her own voice. Animals did not smell of oil.

She moved the way the trail itself moved, a few steps at a time, stopping between the roots, letting the dark settle back around her after each step. The smell grew stronger near the place where the covered trail bent — the trail her mother erased every single time, dragging branches across soft earth, pressing leaves back into place, leaving jungle and only jungle behind.

Yara crouched. The moon gave her almost nothing under the canopy, but she did not need much. A hunter reads the ground with fingers as much as eyes.

The drag marks were there. She could feel their shallow lines.

And pressed down straight through them, deep in the wet earth, was a boot print.

It was not a Vey'kar print. No one in Kijani wore boots. It was not an old print either — its edges were still sharp, and no rain had softened it yet. A heavy man, by the depth of it. A careful man, by where he had put his weight, out at the edge of the trail, where someone who did not want to be followed would walk.

Three steps further on, she found the second print. That one was full of dark water, its edges already crumbling. Older. Days older.

He had not found the trail once, by luck.

He had come back.

The thought settled in her chest like something swallowed wrong. Once could be a lost man, wandering. Twice was a hunter, returning to a trail he meant to use again — and Yara knew, with a cold, clear certainty that made her fingers numb around the knife, that he would come a third time.

Above her head, something clicked.

Yara froze. It was a small sound, neat and mechanical, nothing like a beak or a claw or a branch giving way. Slowly, she raised her face. Strapped to the trunk at head height, wrapped in bark cloth someone had taken care to match to the tree, was a dark box with a glass eye. As she watched, deep inside the glass, a tiny red light blinked once — patient, fed, and awake.

It was a camera. It had been watching the trail. Watching everyone who used the trail. Watching, perhaps, her.

Yara’s mouth went dry. She did not move, though every part of her wanted to — to climb, to tear it down, to run. One hand had pressed flat against the trunk without her deciding it, the bark damp and rough under her palm. How many nights, she thought. How many of us has it seen? Her stomach turned over, slow and cold.

Slowly, she lifted her other hand toward the box — not to touch, not quite, her fingers trembling in the dark.

Oya did not move, or look down at Yara. Her voice was low and flat, with all the feeling pressed down hard underneath it. “Do not touch it.”

Oya's voice came out of the dark so close behind her that Yara's markings flared before she could stop them. Her mother stood between the roots with no light on her at all, and Yara had not heard her come — she never did. Oya was looking not at Yara, and not at the prints, but up at the glass eye, and her face was doing something Yara had never seen it do before.

Her mother was afraid. Not for herself. Yara understood that in the same instant, the way she sometimes understood people before they spoke. For all of it. For the temple and the tree shelters and the twenty souls sleeping behind them, who believed the green place would keep them.

Yara let her raised hand fall to her side. She looked at her mother, steadied her breathing, and tried again, quieter. “How long?” Yara asked quietly. She swallowed, her throat tight, her markings brightening in spite of her. “How long has he been watching us?”

Oya reached out, very slowly, and closed her hand around Yara's wrist — not hard, just certain, the way she had held it as a child at the edge of high places.

Oya was quiet for a long moment. Something tired, and guilty, moved behind her calm — there, and gone — and she did not look at Yara as she answered. “Long enough,” she said, “to learn the way in.” Her thumb pressed once against Yara’s wrist, and stilled.

Behind them, far below through the trees, the shielded fires of Kijani burned small and steady, the way they always did, as if nothing in the world could find them.

Oya let go of her wrist and turned to face her. Her markings, dark all this time, brightened just a little — enough for Yara to see her face plainly: worried, and trying hard not to show how much. “Go and wake Jabari,” Oya said. Her voice dropped lower, and she waited for Yara’s small, shaky nod before she went on. “Quietly. And Yara — from tonight, no one walks the green place alone.”

Yara nodded again, not trusting her voice, and turned toward the fires below, feeling the glass eye at her back.

The trail disappeared behind them on purpose.

Yara noticed it the third time it happened. Her mother walked a few steps into a wall of green, pressed a broad leaf back into place with the flat of her hand, and dragged a fallen branch across the soft earth where their feet had been. Just like that, there was no path. There had never been a path. Anyone coming after them would find jungle, and only jungle. Yara shifted her pack higher on her shoulders and watched her mother's hands work - unhurried, thorough, leaving nothing for strangers' eyes. Part of her was impatient to arrive. Another part, quieter, understood: this slow care was how her mother said she would keep them safe, without ever saying it aloud.

Yara let out a slow breath through her nose, her shoulders dropping under the pack. "Again?" Yara said. It came out flatter and more tired than she meant it to, and she pressed her lips together at once, embarrassed by how childish she sounded.

"Again," Oya said, without turning round. "And again after that. A trail that stays is a door. We do not leave doors open." Oya's hands never stopped moving as she spoke. Her voice was even, almost gentle, but her markings held a steady, watchful brightness in the shade, and Yara knew better than to argue with that brightness.

They had been walking since before the sun was properly up. Yara's legs ached in a way she was too proud to mention. The pack on her back was not even the heaviest one - Oya carried the heavy load, tools and salt and dried meat wrapped in leaves, and Oya was not breathing hard at all. That was the difference between them, one of many, and Yara had been counting the differences all morning the way she always did when she was tired. Under the tiredness sat something she did not say either: if she complained, she would sound human in exactly the way Jabari expected, and she would rather ache in silence than give him that.

Her mother was tall, even for a Vey'kar, and moved through the trees like the trees made room for her. The markings on her arms glowed faintly in the shade, gold under warm bronze skin, brightening whenever she stopped to listen. Yara's own markings were paler. If she squinted at the back of her hand, she could just about see them. If a human looked, they would probably see nothing at all. Jabari would have something to say about that. Jabari always had something to say. Yara looked down at her own hands again, flexing her fingers in the dim light, willing the faint lines to show themselves. They stayed pale. Her chest tightened with the old, familiar wish - to look, just once, unmistakably like her mother's daughter.

She pushed the thought away and concentrated on the jungle instead.

Even tired, she loved this part. The jungle talked, if you knew how to hear it, and Yara - whatever else she was - had inherited the hearing. Rain was coming, hours away yet, but she could smell it, clean and sharp above the rich smell of wet earth. Water ran somewhere to her left, over stone. Something small and quick was moving through the branches high above them, and something much larger was standing very still a long way off to the right, being a tree so hard that it gave itself away. She lifted her face as she walked, breathing it all in slowly, and for a moment the tiredness fell away. Out here, at least, her body knew what it was doing, even if she did not always know what she was.

Yara froze mid-step, one foot still raised, her ears pricked and her heart suddenly loud in her chest. "Deer?" she whispered. The whisper came out eager - too eager, she knew even as she said it.

"Boar," Oya said. "An old one. He knows we are here and he has decided we are not worth standing up for. He is right. Keep walking." Oya did not even turn her head. She said it the way she said most true things, calmly and without doubt, one hand resting lightly on a tree trunk as she listened past Yara's hearing. There was no teasing in it, only certainty, and Yara felt both steadied and small at once.

Yara grinned at the back of her mother's head. Her senses were strong - but her mother's were stronger, the way a river is stronger than a stream, and one day, Oya had promised, her own would deepen. One day. A lot of Yara's life lived in one day. Hope rose in her anyway, warm and stubborn - that one day she would hear a jungle the way her mother heard it, and no one would be able to tell her she belonged only halfway.

The light changed first. That was how she knew they were close before she was told. The green above them grew thicker, layer on layer of canopy, until the morning came through soft and gold and broken into pieces. The air tasted different too - woodsmoke, very faint, and cooking, and people. Her heart kicked. Her steps quickened without her deciding to hurry. Whatever waited under that canopy, she wanted to see it with her own eyes at last.

Yara stopped dead and lifted her head, nostrils flaring as she breathed in deep, her markings prickling faintly with excitement. "Oya," she said. "I can smell a fire."

"Good," her mother said, and for the first time all morning, Yara heard her smile. "Then smell this too. No light leaves this place. No smoke rises straight. Everything Kijani does, it does under cover. Remember it." As she spoke, Oya reached out and rested her palm briefly over Yara's hand, warm and sure, guiding her attention the way she guided everything - by touch as much as by words. Pride warmed Yara's chest, followed quickly by the weight of the warning inside it.

They stepped between two great roots, taller than Yara herself, and the jungle opened. Yara caught her breath and stopped without meaning to, one hand pressed flat against the great root beside her, as if she needed to touch something solid.

Kijani was smaller than she had pictured, and more beautiful, and it undid her completely.

She had imagined a camp. What she found was a village being born.

The temple stood at the centre of a wide clearing, old stone rising step above step, its edges trimmed with gold that caught what little sun reached the ground. Who had built it, and when, nobody had ever told her - it was simply there, older than everyone, as if the jungle had grown up around it and decided to keep it. Torches burned at its base even in daylight, small steady flames shielded under the canopy, and the smoke from them broke apart in the leaves and never rose in a line. Yara turned in a slow circle in the middle of the clearing, taking it in piece by piece, her mouth slightly open. She did not know where to look first, and that not-knowing felt like awe.

Around the temple, the village was still taking shape. Two shelters stood finished in the great trees, platforms of woven wood lashed high in the branches, with ladders and rope bridges strung between them. A third was half built, its frame open to the sky like ribs. Down on the ground, near the river that slid bright along the edge of the clearing, a hut stood with bunches of herbs drying under its roof. Yara could smell them from here - bitter, green, medicinal.

There were so few people. A family working together on the half-built shelter. An old hunter sharpening a spear by the temple steps. Two small children who stopped their game to stare at Yara with enormous amber eyes, the way children stare at anything new. Yara folded her arms tightly across herself, suddenly aware of every eye. She was the stranger here, whatever her mother called her, and the thought sat cold in her stomach.

Listening, Yara felt the smallness of the place stop frightening her. In her mother's voice it was not a camp at all. Yara stored that away. She stored away a lot of things her mother said. Oya spoke rarely and wasted nothing, and Yara had learned young that a sentence of her mother's could be unpacked for days.

That first evening, before the food, before anything, Oya took her up the temple steps so the whole clearing could see them, and the village - all of it, perhaps twenty people - gathered below in the torchlight. Yara's face burned. Her markings, traitors, brightened with it. She stood stiffly at her mother's side, hands clasped hard in front of her so no one would see them shake, and tried to stand the way a future keeper of rules was supposed to stand.

"The rules are not new," Oya said. Her voice was not loud. It did not need to be. "But my daughter will hear them where everyone can hear them, because one day she will be the one who keeps them." Oya stood very still as she spoke, chin lifted, her gaze moving slowly across the faces below, giving the words time to land. Yara watched her mother's hands hang loose and steady at her sides, and envied that steadiness with her whole heart.

Yara's stomach dropped straight through the stone steps.

That is the first law on this world, above every other. We keep our markings covered beyond the tree line. We dim our eyes. We take only the trails, and we close the trails behind us. She felt the law settle over the clearing - and over her most of all.

She wanted, very suddenly and very badly, not to let any of them down. It was a frightening feeling, wanting something that big. "And if I am seen?" she asked, because someone had to ask it and it might as well be the person most likely to do it. "Out there. Away from here." Her voice wavered only at the end. She lifted her chin and made herself hold her mother's gaze, even as her hands twisted together behind her back.

Is that understood?" Oya's voice hardened on the last question, and her markings brightened sharply in the torchlight - not anger at Yara, Yara understood, but fear, quickly mastered and hidden behind command. The sight of it frightened Yara more than the words had.

Yara's throat clicked as she swallowed. "Yes." The single word came out small and tight, and she hated how small it sounded in front of everyone.

"By me alone is not enough." Oya turned her by the shoulder, gently, so she faced the clearing. "Tell them."

Yara swallowed. "I understand," she said, to twenty watching faces. "I will keep the secret." Inside, her heart was hammering so hard she was sure Zola, somewhere below, could hear it.

Zola.

There was no pity in her touch, only quiet knowing, and Yara felt seen in a way that was both comforting and unsettling - as if Amara already knew things about her she had not said aloud.

Yara straightened on the spot, chin up, markings flickering with indignation. "I will not be late." The words came out firmer than she felt, aimed at Zola but meant, mostly, for herself.

Amara's mouth softened. "No," she said. "I do not think you will be. Eat, child. Then sleep. Kijani will still be here in the morning - that is rather the point of it." Amara pressed the bowl more firmly into Yara's hands and gave her fingers a brief, motherly squeeze before letting go. Warmth spread through Yara that had nothing to do with the food.

The face at the edge of the torchlight came to her after the meal, when the fire had burned low and the clearing was full of that soft, full-bellied quiet that Yara had not known she was homesick for until she heard it. Yara's shoulders tightened when she saw who was coming toward her. She set her bowl down carefully, buying herself a moment, her appetite gone.

Jabari was an elder, in his late sixties, a traditional hunter of the old world and - long ago, before Yara was born - Oya's own teacher. He was not a large man, for a Vey'kar. He did not need to be. He looked at people the way ice looks at a river.

"Walk with me," he said. It was not a request in any useful sense. Jabari did not wait to see if she followed. He simply turned and walked, hands clasped behind his back, certain she would come. After a heartbeat, stomach sinking, Yara did.

They walked the edge of the clearing, where the training ground lay open and empty in the dark. Yara could see every root and stone of it, her night eyes open wide. That, at least, she could do properly.

"Your mother speaks of you as an heir," Jabari said. "I taught your mother. I know what a hunter looks like before the making, and I know what softness looks like too. He kept his eyes ahead as he spoke, never once looking at her, and somehow that was worse than anger would have been. Yara walked beside him with her fists clenched at her sides, each word landing like a small, cold stone in her chest.

Heat went through Yara so fast her markings flared, which was the worst possible answer and they both knew it.

"When your trial comes," Jabari said mildly, watching them glow, "ask for an easy one, princess. There is no shame in knowing what you are." He said it almost gently, and the gentleness stung worst of all. Yara looked down at her traitorous, glowing arms and curled her fingers in, as if she could hide the light in her fists.

He walked back to the fire. Yara stood in the dark at the edge of the training ground for a long time, looking at nothing, until she was quite sure her face would behave, and her markings had gone back to their usual traitorous pale.

There is no shame in knowing what you are.

She just wished she knew.

She could not sleep in a new place. She never could. So when the village had gone quiet and the torches in the clearing had burned down to coals, Yara got up from the woven mat in the corner of Oya's hut that was hers now - hers, in a home, in a village, a sentence she kept trying out - and began, very quietly, to unpack. She moved slowly, listening with every breath for her mother's breathing across the hut, half hoping Oya was asleep and half hoping she was not.

Her own things first. Knife. Cloak, folded down small - the light-weave cloak Amara had woven, which bent the light around its wearer the way heat bends the air, and which Yara could use for about as long as she could hold her breath. A change of clothes. Then the bottom of the pack, where her mother had put the things from their old place that were too important to leave.

Her fingers found something she did not know.

It was a charm of carved bone, no longer than her thumb, worn smooth as river stone. A hunter's charm - the kind an elder carves and a healer blesses, that hums, faintly, when prey is near. Even in her hand, at rest, it seemed to hold a warmth the other bone things in the pack did not hold. She had never seen it. She would have remembered this.

Around its edge, in tiny, careful cuts, someone had carved letters. Human letters. Three words.

Jonah Noah Moses.

Yara read them four times.

Her father had had a human name once, before Oya - before everything. In the village he was Ayo, the name her mother had given him, a man's name in her mother's mouth. He had been gone so long that Yara owned only three facts about him: he had laughed at his own terrible Vey'kar clicks, he had loved her mother, and nobody - nobody - would tell her the rest.

"Mother," she said, into the dark of the hut, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Whose is this?" In her lap, her fingers tightened around the charm until its carved edge pressed into her palm. She did not let go of it. She was not sure she could have, even if her mother ordered her to.

Oya was awake. Somehow, Yara understood that her mother had been awake the whole time, listening to her unpack, waiting - or hoping - or dreading. For a long while, she did not answer at all.

Oya did not look up. In the dark her markings were bright enough to see by, though her voice stayed flat and quiet. "Put it back," Oya said at last, quietly. "And sleep, Yara. It is your first night in Kijani." Yara heard what the flatness was holding back - a grief her mother would not name - and it silenced every question crowding her tongue.

Yara put the charm back, exactly where she had found it. Then she lay down on her mat in the dark, in the new village, in her new home, with her father's name buzzing in her head like the charm had tried to hum in her hand. Her eyes stayed open a long time in the dark, listening to the new village breathe around her - hope, homesickness and hurt all tangled together, and underneath them, stubborn as a heartbeat, curiosity about her father's name.

Somebody, she thought, was going to tell her. One day.

That was the first surprise of the morning. The jungle at night was not a darker version of the jungle by day. It was a different country that happened to use the same trees.

Yara had known that all her life, the way you know a song. Knowing it and being given a share of it to guard were not the same thing. Out beyond the tree line, with Kijani's fire gone behind the leaves and the canopy shut over them like a hand, her senses opened the way they were meant to open - not in a flare, not in a flood, but steadily, the way Amara had promised they would if she stopped gripping them.

Out, long, through the nose. Put down the feeling that feeds it.

The river first, always, the great low note under everything. Insects in layers, stitching the dark together. A night bird, offended, far to the left. Water over stone. The breathing of the girl beside her, who moved - even Zola, especially Zola - in a quiet so complete it had texture, like cloth.

Their markings stayed down. That was the first discipline of the night walk, and it was not for hiding from prey. It was for hiding from eyes. Out here, a glow was a door left open, and Yara's mother did not leave doors open, and so neither, tonight, would Yara. When excitement rose in her - at a scent, at a sound she could not name fast enough - she felt the shimmer climb her arms like water climbing a bank, and she breathed, and let it sink, and was absurdly proud the third time it sank without her having to think about sinking it.

Zola's shoulder brushed hers, warm in the dark, and her whisper came with a grin in it. "You are doing the healer's face," Zola whispered.

Yara kept her eyes forward, annoyed that it was true, her markings flickering once before she breathed them down again. "I am doing the healer's breathing."

"It comes with a face. I am just telling you, in case queens are not allowed that face." A pause, in which Zola read a whole paragraph of ground by the soles of her feet, then nudged Yara very gently with an elbow, fond and teasing at once. "It is working, though. You are almost not a lantern."

They worked the trail the way the morning's line had worked, except that now there was no lesson in it, and that changed every step. Zola went first, a few paces ahead, spear carried low across her body, head turning slowly from ground to trees and back. Yara walked behind her, close enough to see Zola's hand when it rose, far enough back that one trap, or one fall, could not take them both. Yara kept one hand slightly raised, ready to signal, and the other loose at her side, feeling the night air move over her skin for any change. Hand signs did the talking. Stop. Scent. Listen. Down. Twice they froze for animals that were only animals - once a heavy, rootling shape that grunted and moved off, and Yara thought of the sow, somewhere out in the green dark with her three striped shadows pressed close, and hoped, with her whole dimmed heart, that the night stayed boring for her too.

Behind them, every few hundred paces, they closed the trail. A pressed leaf pressed back. A branch drawn across soft earth. A footprint - never theirs for long - scuffed out with the side of a foot and leaf mould. Yara had done this work that morning understanding it from the other side. Tonight she understood it from underneath: it was not tidying. It was the village, erasing its own name from the ground, over and over, so that no stranger could ever read it aloud.

Halfway to the gully, Zola stopped without a sign, which was itself a sign, and the worst kind.

Yara went still beside her and waited, because one of the things the day had taught her was that Zola's silences were information too.

Zola's hand came up, fingers spread - stop - and her nose lifted, her whole body gone alert. "Smell that," Zola breathed.

Yara let the dark come in through her nose in layers, the way Amara sorted herbs: river, rot, wet leaf, animal, the clean green smell of a vine cut hours ago and already healing. And under it - faint, wrong, and instantly familiar, because she had held it in her hands that morning and it had not left her since - metal. Not much. Not near. A rumour of metal, carried on the wet air, from up the trail.

Yara's throat closed round the memory of it in her hands. "Wire," she said, barely aloud.

"Or the place where wire was." Zola's head turned slowly, reading, her voice kept low and flat so it would not carry. "Ground says nothing new has walked our trail. Whatever that is, it is off it, or above it, or old." She looked at Yara sideways. "So. Read him, then. That is your side of the work. Who is he, princess, this man of ours?"

Yara almost snapped that he was not theirs. Then she understood the question properly, and it steadied her, the way Jabari's cruelty on the training ground had steadied her - by giving her fear a shape to stand next to.

"He is patient," she said, slowly, her breath evening out as the shape of him came clear in her mind, thinking of the bent sapling, the chosen place above water. "He does not hunt the way we hunt. He sets a thing and leaves it to be hungry for him. He trusts his tools more than his feet." Cold anger rose in her as she spoke - anger at a man who could sit still and let hunger do his hunting for him - and her markings prickled with it until she breathed it down. Her fingers tightened, then loosened, as she thought of the vine, cut through in one clean stroke to clear the line. "He keeps his line tidy. Like a path he owns. He thinks this part of the jungle is his, because he has marked it and no one has argued." She stopped. "And he comes back. A man who only wanted the setting would not need the line kept. He comes back to read what it caught."

Zola was quiet a moment, studying her in the dark, the teasing gone out of her face. "That is a frightening amount to get from two wires and a smell."

Yara lifted one shoulder, a tight little shrug, and let out a breath she had been holding. "It is what Jabari said. Think like the one who laid it."

"Hm." Zola started forward again, slower now, placing each foot like a word she could take back. "Remind me never to lay a trail in front of you. I would come home and find you knowing my grandmother's secrets and what I had for breakfast." She shook her head as she said it, half admiring, half unsettled.

"Roots," Yara said, and felt a small, tired smile pull at her mouth in the dark. "Same as me. It was not a hard trail."

Zola's grin flashed in the dark, brief as a firefly, and was put away again - because they were coming, now, down the shallow slope Yara remembered in her knees before she remembered it in her mind, to the gully where a young deer had stood that morning spending the last of itself on being stone.

The gully was empty, and the empty was wrong in a way that took Yara a moment to untangle - until she realised she had been expecting the sapling.

It was still there, of course. Trees did not leave. But the strain had gone out of it. Jabari had held it down that morning while the wire hung slack, and afterwards it had sprung back only part of the way, still bent a little towards the ground, like a bow half-drawn. Now it stood straighter. Taller than she remembered. As if something had eased it the rest of the way home - recently, and by hand.

Zola was already crouched at its foot, not touching, reading with her eyes the way other people read with fingers.

Zola's fingertips hovered over the bark, not touching, and her voice dropped until it was almost part of the dark. "He has been here," she said, very quietly. "Since us. Since morning."

They found the rest of it the way you find a sentence, word by word, in an order that only frightened them once it was whole.

First, the watching place: on the far bank, a fan of ferns had been pressed flat - not broken, not cut, pressed, over hours, by someone sitting very still in the one place where the gully, the game trail, and the water could all be watched at once without being seen. Yara lowered herself, carefully, into the shape of it, and felt the jungle arrange itself in front of her exactly as it must have for him. The snare had hung there. The water shone there. Anything thirsty would cross that ground.

Yara's hand rose to her mouth; her markings gave one sick flare and she forced them down. "He sat here while the deer was - " She could not finish it.

Zola reached towards her, stopped herself - orders - and let the gentleness do the work instead. "No," Zola said, from the earth, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Look. The pressing is old under the new. He sat here many times before the deer. This is not a place a man finds once, princess. This is a place a man keeps."

Second, the line itself. They walked it the way Oya had ordered: counting, touching nothing. Where the first snare had hung there was bare sapling and a bright new scar in the bark - the mark of wire taken off in a hurry by the man who had put it there, and found his trap gone and another man's work in its place. Where the second snare had hung, the same. He had read his line, exactly as Yara had said he would. He had found it emptied. He had taken what was left of it away with him.

And third - fifty paces further on than they had walked that morning, past the place where the hunt had ended and the word had gone home - a third snare, sprung.

Yara crouched beside Zola without touching it, knees in the damp mould, leaning in until she could see the feathers clearly. It hung open and empty at ankle height, its loop sprung shut on nothing but air and a scatter of grey-brown feathers, very small, pressed into the leaf mould underneath. A small, stubborn hope moved in her chest as she looked at them: whatever had been caught here had not stayed caught. Whatever it had caught had not been there when he came to read his line. Something - a bird, a small quick thing of the ground - had got out of it, or been got out of it, before he arrived. Or had been taken out of it by something else entirely, and carried away into a jungle that did not explain itself.

Zola's hand shot up, sharp, real fear under the order in her eyes. "Do not," Zola said, before Yara had even moved. "Orders. Touch nothing we do not have to. It counts as changed, princess. That is enough. It gets to be a number in your report, not a thing in your hands."

It was on the way back up out of the gully that the night offered Yara the thing she would have to refuse.

A sign. Not on their trail - across it. A line of bent grass and disturbed mould, coming down off the far bank from the watching place and heading away, east and north, into the deep green where no village trail ran at all. It was not old, like the pressing of the ferns. It was not hours old, like the eased sapling. Down in the damp hollow where the air sat still, Zola crouched over it for a long time, and when she stood up her face had lost every trace of the girl who made jokes about breakfast.

Her jaw was tight, her markings flaring once before she forced them down. "That is tonight," she said. "That may be this hour. He read his line, he found it robbed, and he went that way. Looking." A beat. "Or leaving. I cannot swear which, and I will not lie to you on a night trail, princess. The ground there will tell us. Fifty paces would tell us."

Fifty paces.

Yara stood with the dark pressing in and felt the pull of it go through her like a hook finding cloth. Fifty paces to know whether a man was walking towards her village in the dark, this hour, while her mother sat trusting that the word would come home first. Fifty paces was nothing. Zola would go - she could feel Zola waiting for the word, ready to go, ready in fact to be told not to go, because Zola was standing in the dark asking with her whole body for Yara to be the one who decided.

Word before pride. Her mother's voice, on the temple steps, in the low red light. There is no one out there to say dead and let you begin again.

In her mind she saw her mother in the hut doorway, waiting. She saw the village behind its closed trail, children asleep in the shelters, trusting that the dark would be watched for them. Fear said follow him, before he found them. Pride said follow him, and come home with the answer. Both voices were hers, and she heard them clearly, and that was how she knew not to obey either one. And underneath that, older and quieter, Jabari on the training ground: Anger is loud, princess. This was not anger. But it shone the same way. It wanted the same thing - to move now, and be right later.

Yara's hands fisted at her sides; her markings flared, bright and wanting, and sank as she breathed through it. "No," Yara said. It came out rough, because it cost. "We were not sent to meet him. We were sent to know. And we know." She made herself say the rest of it properly, like a report, because a report was a thing she could hold. "He came back. He read his line. He found it emptied. He may still be looking. That is the word, and the word goes home first, whole, and fast. If we follow him and he finds us instead, then the word never gets home at all, and Kijani learns about him from his mouth, not ours."

Zola let out a breath she had clearly been saving, and a shaky ghost of her grin came back. "I hate it when the orders are right," she said. "It is so much worse than when they are stupid."

Yara nodded once, towards the trail, her own breathing steadying as the decision settled. "Mark it," Yara said. "Our way. Where only we will read it."

So Zola went down on one knee at the edge of the trail and made, in thirty seconds and with two fingers and a broken twig laid just so, the smallest sign Yara had ever seen her make: a Vey'kar trail mark, old as the temple, that said to any hunter of Kijani who ever passed this way again - here. This far. No further, on this night, without the word. To anyone else alive it was leaf mould and a fallen twig, and it would be gone by rain.

Then they went home the way a word should go home: without stopping, without shining, and with the trail closing behind them like water.

Oya took their report standing, in the dark of her own hut doorway, with the coils of dead wire on the floor between them like a second, smaller fire.

Yara gave it as she had rehearsed it on the walk home, in order, with the numbers in it. Two snares taken up by their owner. A watching place kept, not found, above the gully. A third snare further on, sprung and empty, small feathers under it. A fresh sign leading away east and north. Not followed. Marked.

Oya went very still; her eyes searched Yara's face, looking for the wanting underneath the words. "Not followed," Oya repeated, quiet.

Yara stood straight under that gaze, keeping her hands deliberately unclenched at her sides. "The orders were word first," Yara said. "It may have been him leaving. It may have been him looking. Fifty paces would have told us, and we did not spend them."

Her mother's face, in the low light, did one of its complicated things and was still. For a heartbeat, though, Yara saw through it: relief first, flooding in, then pride, and underneath both the same guilt she had glimpsed at the fire - because Oya had sent her own daughter into that dark, and had sat and waited, and could not have done otherwise. Oya gathered it all away, the way she gathered everything, and was the queen again.

Oya turned slowly to Zola, one eyebrow rising, disbelief and something warmer underneath it. "Zola," Oya said. "You let her refuse you a trail. In the dark. Willingly."

Zola's hands hung loose and tired at her sides; she met the queen's eyes without flinching. "She refused herself first, my queen," Zola said, and there was not a joke anywhere in her. "I only had to stand there and watch it cost her. It is a very educational sight."

Jabari had come in without anyone hearing him, the way weather arrives, and stood at the edge of the doorway light with his spear in his hand. He had walked the west trail alone and come home with nothing to report, and that made him the most dangerous listener in the hut.

His grip shifted on the spear, a small, approving sound buried in his throat. "She left a mark," he said.

Zola lifted her chin, defensive on Yara's behalf, though her voice stayed respectful. "Our mark, Elder," Zola said. "Small as a lie. Rain takes it."

"And she closed the trail behind her, all the way home, at speed, in the dark." He was not asking Zola. His arms were folded, his chin down, his whole stance one long disapproval - but it was a testing kind of disapproval now, the kind that watched to be proved wrong. He was, Yara realised, reading her - the mud to the knee, the cloak still settled right on her shoulders, the markings, even now, held low and steady. "Twice in one day the girl has come home empty-handed," he said to Oya, "and twice she has brought back the thing the village actually needed. I begin to see a pattern in it, and I do not care for patterns." He turned to go. He stopped, because stopping at doorways to deliver one last sentence was, Yara suspected, the whole reason Jabari had doorways. "East trail. Tomorrow night. She leads it. A leader who will not be led by her own wanting is - " He searched, visibly, for a colder word, and failed to find one he would spend. " - less of a danger than I reported at dawn."

And he was gone, into a dark he needed no eyes for.

Oya looked at her daughter for a long moment.

Oya laid a hand on Yara's shoulder then, and left it there, its weight warm and steady. "You lead it," she said. "Zola walks with you - as your tracker, Yara, not your keeper. That is different, and you will both feel the difference by midnight tomorrow." She bent, picked up one coil of wire, held it a moment, then set it back down gently, as if it were asleep. "Sleep now. Both of you. A village that is being looked for does not get to be tired in the morning."

Yara slept the way a held breath rests: lightly, and all in one piece, and she woke before dawn with Zola's voice already in the doorway of the hut, arguing happily with somebody about whose turn it was to be slow.

The village woke around her - no. She had learned this on her first morning, and she said it right this time, even in her head. Kijani did not wake. It continued. The night watch came down from the trees and handed the dark over to the louder people, and among the people coming down, walking the last of the river trail with the empty, ordinary hands of a hunter who had found nothing and closed everything, were hunters who nodded to her as they passed.

Not the nod you give a princess on temple steps while her mother is talking. A shorter one. A working one. The nod you give someone who was out in the same dark you were out in, doing a share of the same work.

She carried that nod down to Amara's hut to have her shoulder seen to, and Amara took one look at her face, hands on her hips, her sharp eyes softening with the quiet knowing of a woman who had already read the whole night in Yara's pulse and her held-low markings, and then at her wrist, where the pulse was, and said, "Same girl who left. Slightly more of her. Sit. Eat. You will tell me all of it while I pretend not to be listening, which is the healer's custom, and then you will tell me the parts you left out of the report."

Yara looked away, both hands tightening at her sides, her markings dimming in a way that gave her away. "There were no parts left out of the report."

"There is always a part left out of a report, child. It is the part where you were frightened." Amara set a bowl in her hands. "That part is mine. Eat first. Fear keeps." She said it gently, and let her hand linger a moment on Yara's as she passed the bowl, warm and matter-of-fact.

Later - much later, when the light was full and the clearing sounded, blessedly, like nothing more alarming than a village building itself - Yara stood at the edge of the training ground and let the morning be ordinary on purpose, the way her mother had let the evening be ordinary, and understood she had been handed a second inheritance. Not the crown. Not yet. The calm. The work of keeping twenty frightened people fed and building and laughing, while somewhere out in the green a patient man sat in a place he kept, read his emptied line, and wondered what kind of thing walked his jungle at night, took his traps apart without breaking them, freed what they caught, and left no trail home.

And she would be out there when he did - not only the girl who had been tested at dawn, but someone the village now nodded to in the working way. That thought steadied her, and frightened her, in equal measure. He would come back. That was the truest thing she knew about him, truer than his patience, truer than his tidy line. A man does not abandon a question he has paid that much sitting-still for. He had lost two snares and found a mystery, and a mystery, to a man like that, was worth more than a deer.

He would come back to read his line again.

And next time, Yara thought - not with fear, exactly, and not without it either - next time the line would not be all he was reading for.

Before the night came, she took the bone charm from the bottom of the pack and slipped it onto her belt. She would not leave it in the pack tonight.

“It is not watching you,” Zola whispered back, and her whisper came with a grin in it, though Yara could not see it, only hear it. “It is waiting to see if you are worth watching. Yara breathed out, slow, the way Amara had taught her, and felt her markings dim, and her senses settle, and the jungle come back into focus around her. The river first, always, the great low note under everything. Insects in layers, near and far, stitching the dark together. A night bird, offended about something, far to the left. Water over stone. The breathing of the girl behind her, who moved in a quiet so complete it had texture, like cloth.

She was doing the healer’s breathing, she thought, and the thought made her smile a little, in the dark, where no one could see it. She was doing the healer’s breathing, and the healer’s face, and she was leading a trail, and her mother had promised her one true thing about her father, and for a moment, walking first in the dark, she felt like all of her at once: hunter and human, daughter and leader, afraid and steady, and it did not tear her in two the way she had always feared it would.

The trail bent down towards the gully, and Yara slowed, and put her hand up, fingers spread, the way Zola had taught her, and Zola stopped behind her without a sound.

“Here,” Yara said, very quietly, and her voice was steady, and she was proud of that, and frightened by it, both at once. “This is where it was. The snare gully. Smell.”

Zola’s nose lifted, and her whole body went alert, the way it did when the ground had something to say. “Wire,” she breathed. “And something else. Something new.”

Yara crouched, and let her fingers hover over the ground without touching it, and let her senses do the work they had been taught to do. The smell of wire, yes, faint and cold, and under it, something she had not smelled before: a sharp, sweet smell, like dried meat left too long in the sun, and the smell of oil, cleaned metal, a smell she did not yet have a name for.

“He has been back,” she said, and her throat closed round the words, and she made herself say them anyway, clearly, so that Zola would hear them true. “Since last night. The line has been read.”

The snare was new. That was the first thing Yara understood, and the second thing she understood was that it had been set by a hand that was not in a hurry.

It hung in the same place the third snare had hung, in the narrow part of the gully where an animal had to step, and it was wire, bright and clean, and it was baited: a small piece of dried meat, tied with a short length of vine, hanging just above the loop, so that whatever came for the meat would put its foot in the wire first.

“He reset it,” Zola said, and her voice was low and flat, the way it went when she was frightened and did not want to show it. “He came back, he saw his snares were gone, and he set a new one. In the same place. He is not afraid of us. He does not know we are here, or he does not care.”

“He knows something is here,” Yara said, slowly, her breath evening out as the shape of him came clear in her mind, the way it had come clear the night before. “Think like the one who laid it. That is what Jabari said. He lost two snares, and he did not leave. He came back, and he set a better one, with bait, because he wants to know what took them. He is patient. He is tidy. He is certain, and he is not afraid to be certain in the dark.”

She looked at the ground around the snare, and read it the way she had been taught to read it, slowly, and out loud, so that Zola could hear her thinking, and so that she could hear herself think.

“One boot,” she said, pointing without touching, her finger hovering over the print pressed into the soft earth at the edge of the gully. “Here, and here. The same boot as last night, I think, the same size, the same tread, but deeper this time. He was carrying something heavy when he came back. A bag, maybe. Or a trap, bigger than the snare. And here —” she moved her hand, and Zola leaned in close beside her, their shoulders touching in the dark, “— here he knelt. The ground is pressed flat, in the shape of a knee. He knelt to set the wire, and he stayed kneeling a while, because he was listening. He was listening for us.”

Zola was quiet a moment, studying her in the dark, the teasing gone out of her face. “That is a frightening amount to get from one boot and a piece of meat,” she said, and her voice was soft, and admiring, and a little bit afraid, all at once.

“It is what Jabari said,” Yara said, and she lifted one shoulder, a tight little shrug, and let out a breath she had been holding. “Think like the one who laid it. And I am thinking like him, and what I am thinking is that he will come back again, and soon, and next time he will not only read his line. Next time he will wait beside it.”

She stood up, slowly, and her knees ached from crouching, and her markings were bright along her arms, and she breathed them down, deliberately, the way Amara had taught her, because a leader could not be a lantern on a trail where a man was watching.

“Close it,” she said, and her voice was steady, and it was her voice, and it was also, she realised, her mother’s voice, the way Oya’s voice sounded when she gave an order she wished she did not have to give. “We close it, the way we were told to. We take the wire, we take the bait, and we leave nothing for him to read but an empty gully. And we leave him a mark.”

“A mark?” Zola asked, and her eyebrows went up in the dark.

“A Vey’kar mark,” Yara said, and she bent, and with the tip of her knife she cut a small, clean sign into the bark of the tree above the gully: three short lines, and a circle, the sign for watched, and for warned, and for we see you. “Only Kijani can read it. But he will see that something was here, and that it took his snare, and that it left him nothing. Let him wonder what it was. Wondering is better than knowing. Knowing is what he wants.”

Zola looked at the mark, and then at Yara, and her grin came back, slow and bright. “You are starting to sound like a queen,” she said, and she said it fondly, and Yara felt her face go warm in the dark.

“I am starting to sound like my mother,” Yara said, and she laughed, quietly, because it was true, and because it frightened her a little, and because it steadied her too. “Do not tell her. She will only say she told me so.”

They had closed the snare, and taken the wire, and left the mark, and they were on their way home, walking the trail back towards the village in the dark, when Yara heard it.

It was not a loud sound. It was the kind of sound that would have meant nothing to anyone who was not listening for it, and everything to someone who was: a twig, snapping, somewhere off the trail to their left, and then, a moment later, a cough.

A human cough. Short, and smothered, and swallowed almost as soon as it had escaped, as if the man who had made it had clapped a hand over his own mouth the instant he heard himself.

Yara froze. Her whole body went still, the way a hunted animal goes still, and then, a heartbeat later, the way a hunter goes still, and she put her hand up, fingers spread, stop, and Zola stopped behind her without a sound, and without a question, because Zola knew that hand, and what it meant.

“He is here,” Yara breathed, and her voice was barely a sound at all, and her markings were bright, so bright that she could see their blue light on the leaves in front of her, and she breathed, slow, and breathed them down again, until the dark closed back over them. “He is out there. To the left. Not on the trail. Off it, in the trees, watching the gully.”

Zola’s hand found Yara’s arm in the dark, and gripped it, hard, and Yara could feel her friend’s fingers trembling, just a little, and her own fingers trembling too, and she understood, with a clarity that frightened her, that this was the moment the orders were for. This was the moment when following would have been easier, and when following would have been wrong.

“We could follow him,” Zola whispered, and her voice was tight with the want of it, and with the fear of it, both at once. “We could follow him, and see where he goes, and see what he carries, and then we would know. Yara, we would know.”

“I know,” Yara said, and her throat was tight, and her heart was beating hard enough that she could feel it in her teeth, and she wanted to follow him too, so much that her feet were already leaning that way, towards the trees, towards the cough, towards the answer. “I want to know too. But the order is the order. Word before pride. Word before everything. If we follow him, and he sees us, then he knows, and then Kijani is not hidden any more. And if we follow him, and he does not see us, we still come home late, and my mother sends the watch out after us, and then there are more of us in the dark, and more of us to be seen.”

She was quiet for a moment, listening, and the jungle was quiet with her, holding its breath, and then she heard it again: a soft sound, further away this time, a footstep, careful and slow, moving away from the gully, back towards the human paths, and she understood that he was leaving, and that he had not seen them, and that he had come, and read his emptied line, and found nothing, and gone.

“He is leaving,” she said, and her voice was steady now, and she was proud of that, and frightened by it, both at once. “Let him go. Let him go home with nothing but a missing snare and a mark he cannot read. That is the word. That is what we bring home.”

Zola was quiet for a long moment, and Yara could feel her friend’s grip loosening on her arm, slowly, reluctantly, and then Zola let out a breath, long and shaky, and laughed, very quietly, in the dark.

“You are a terrible tracker,” Zola whispered, and her voice was fond, and teasing, and relieved, all at once. “A terrible tracker, who will not follow a trail when it is right there in front of her. My grandmother would be ashamed of you. I am going to tell her, when I see her, that you let a human walk away.”

“Tell her,” Yara said, and she started walking again, back towards the village, and her legs were shaking, and her hands were shaking, and she did not hide it from Zola, because Zola was her friend, and friends did not have to hide shaking hands. “Tell her I let him walk away, and that I brought the word home instead. Tell her that is what leading is.”

The fire had burned low by the time they came home, and the village was waiting, and Yara felt the weight of every eye on her as she crossed the clearing, and her markings were bright, and she did not try to dim them, because she was too tired to hide, and because, she realised, she did not want to hide.

Oya was standing on the temple steps, and Jabari was standing at the edge of the steps, and Amara was sitting by the fire, and the whole village was gathered in the clearing, quiet, waiting for the word.

Yara stopped at the foot of the steps, and Zola stopped beside her, half a pace behind her, the way a tracker stands behind a leader, and Yara took a breath, slow, and let it out, slow, and spoke.

“The east trail is closed,” she said, and her voice was steady, and it carried across the clearing, and she was surprised by how steady it was, and how much it sounded like her mother’s voice. “The snare is gone. He reset it, with bait, in the same place, and we took it, and we took the bait, and we left him nothing to read but an empty gully, and a mark only we can read.”

She told them about the boot print, deeper this time, and the kneeling place, and the listening, and the patience, and the tidiness, and the certainty, and as she spoke she watched the village listen, and she watched Oya listen, and she watched Jabari listen, and she saw, in their faces, the thing she had been afraid she would not see: belief.

“And then,” she said, and her voice went quieter, because this was the hardest part, and the part she was proudest of, “we heard him. Off the trail, in the trees. A cough. A footstep. He was watching the gully. He was waiting to see what took his line.”

“And you followed him?” Jabari asked, and his voice was flat, and cold, and it cut across the clearing like a blade. “You heard the man who sets snares in our jungle, and you let him walk away? Tell me, girl. Is that leading, or is that fear?”

The clearing went very slightly quiet, and Yara felt her temper flare, hot and sudden, the way it always did when someone insulted her mother, or her people, or her, and her markings flared with it, bright and blue, and she breathed, slow, and let the feeling be there without feeding it, the way Amara had taught her, and the way Jabari himself had taught her, on the training ground, when he had said to think, and not to burn.

“It is leading,” she said, and her voice was level, and her hands were still at her sides, and she looked at Jabari, and did not look away. “If I had followed him, he would have seen me, or he would not have seen me, and either way I would have come home late, and the watch would have gone out after me, and more of us would have been in the dark to be seen. “She leads the east trail,” Jabari said, and he said it to Oya, and to the village, and not to Yara, and that, Yara understood, was the point: he was not praising her to her face, he was telling the village, so that the village would hear it. “The girl reads the man who walks on it, and she brings the word home, and she does not chase what she cannot catch. Until the trial. The words hung in the clearing, and Yara felt them land in her chest, heavy and bright at once, and she looked at Oya, and Oya looked at her, and Oya’s face did something it almost never did in front of the village: it softened, and it was proud, and it was afraid, both at once, and she nodded, once, slow and certain.

“You did well,” Amara said, at last, and her voice was calm, and patient, and wise, the way it always was, and Yara felt her throat go tight, because praise from Amara was rarer than praise from anyone, and it meant more, because Amara did not give it away lightly. “You did very well, Yara. Your mother is proud of you, even if she will not say it in so many words. She says it in orders, and in trust, and in the east trail being yours. That is how your mother says things.”

“I know,” Yara said, and she looked down at her own hands, and at the scar on Amara’s left hand, pale in the dark, and she asked the question she had been carrying since the fire, and since the night before, and since she was seven years old, really, though she had only just found the words for it. “Amara. Amara was quiet for a long moment, and Yara watched her face in the dark, and saw something move behind her calm, something tired, and guilty, and fond, all at once, and she understood, with a small shock, that Amara knew more than she was saying, and that she had always known more than she was saying, and that the knowing hurt her, the way it hurt Yara.

“Your father,” Amara said, slowly, choosing each word the way she chose herbs, carefully, and with respect for what they could do, “walked a trail like that once. Not with a snare. With a question. He always came back to read it, too. He would sit, and wait, and listen, and he would not chase what he could not catch, because he knew that chasing would only frighten it away. He was patient, Yara. Patient, and tidy, and certain. Like the man on your trail. Like you.”

Yara felt her breath catch, and then settle, and something in her chest stood up straighter, and then sat down again, because the words were a gift, and they were also a question, and she did not know which one they were yet.

“Is that all you will tell me?” she asked, and her voice came out smaller than she meant it to, and she hated that, and went on anyway. “One true thing, Amara. That is all I am asking for. One true thing about him.”

Amara looked at her, and her eyes were kind, and sad, and steady, and she reached out, and laid her hand over Yara’s hand, warm and scarred, and her fingers were shaking, just a little, the way old hands sometimes shake, and Yara held them still, gently, the way she would hold a bird.

“One true thing,” Amara said, softly, so that it was for Yara and not for the river, and not for the night. “He loved your mother. And he loved you. And whatever took him from us, it was not because he stopped. Do you understand me, Yara? Whatever it was, it was not because he stopped loving you.”

Yara nodded, and her throat was too tight to speak, and her markings were bright, and she did not try to dim them, because Amara was her mentor, and her second mother, and she did not have to hide from Amara, and for a moment, sitting by the river in the dark, she felt like she belonged, fully, in both of her worlds at once, and it did not tear her in two.

“Thank you,” she whispered, at last, and her voice was steady, and it was her voice, and it was also, she realised, her father’s voice, the way she remembered it from when she was seven: quiet, and curious, and brave in a quiet way. “Thank you, Amara.”

Yara walked back to her shelter alone, through the sleeping village, and the night was quiet around her, and the temple stood in the middle of Kijani, dark and gold and old, and the fire in the clearing had burned down to coals, and the watch was out on the trails, and the village was safe, for tonight, and she understood, slowly, that this was what leading felt like: not a crown, and not a spear, and not a test passed in front of everyone, but a quiet village, sleeping, because someone she loved had trusted her with the dark, and she had brought the word home.

In her shelter, she took the bone charm from her belt, and held it in her hand, and looked at it in the dark, and felt it hum, once, low and warm, the way it had hummed on the night of the second chapter, when Jabari had called her to the training ground at dawn, and she thought about what her mother had promised her: one true thing, when the east trail was hers by daylight too.

It was hers now, she thought, and her markings flared, bright and blue, and she breathed them down again, slow and steady. It was hers by dark, and it was hers by the village’s word, and it was hers by Jabari’s word, which was the hardest word of all to earn, and tomorrow, when the sun came up, she would go to her mother, and she would ask, and her mother would tell her one true thing about her father, and then she would know a little more, and the not-knowing would be a little smaller, and that, she thought, was how a mystery got solved: not all at once, but one true thing at a time.

And then, because she was seventeen, and because she was tired, and because she was proud, and because she was frightened, all at once, she laughed, quietly, in the dark, and put the charm back in its place, and lay down, and slept, and dreamed, not of snares, and not of the man in the trees, but of a river, and a man sitting beside it, patient and tidy and certain, waiting for a question to come back and be read.

He would come back. That was the truest thing she knew about him, truer than his patience, truer than his tidy line. A man does not abandon a question he has paid that much sitting-still for. He had lost three snares now, and found a mystery, and a mystery, to a man like that, was worth more than a deer.

He would come back to read his line again.

And next time, Yara thought, not with fear, exactly, and not without it either, next time the line would not be all he was reading for. Next time he would bring something bigger, and something heavier, and something that would not fit in a snare. And Kijani would have to be ready. And she would have to be ready. And the trial, whatever it was, would have to come soon, because the village could not wait for ever, and neither could she.

She slept, and the village slept, and the trails were watched, and the east trail was hers.

Yara felt her breath catch, and then settle, and something in her chest stood up straighter, because this human, this stranger, this woman lying half in the river with a twisted ankle, hated the same thing Yara hated, and had lost someone to it, the way Jabari had lost someone to humans, and the way Yara might lose someone, if the man with the snares kept coming back.

“Then we will help you,” Yara said, and she said it before Amara could speak, and before she could think about whether it was wise, or whether it was allowed, or whether it was what a leader was supposed to do, and she said it because it was true, and because it was the only thing she could say, and mean it. “We will help you, Elena Ruiz. That is what we do.”

Amara looked at Yara for a long moment, and Yara could see the warning in her eyes, and the pride in her eyes, both at once, and then Amara nodded, once, slow and certain, and turned back to Elena’s ankle, and her hands were gentle, and her voice was gentle, and Yara understood that Amara had just made a choice too, and that the choice was the same as hers.

“Hold still,” Amara said, and her fingers were careful on Elena’s ankle, feeling the bones, and the swelling, and the heat, and her face was calm, and focused, the way it always was when she was healing. “It is not broken, I think. It is twisted, and swollen, and it will hurt for a while, but it is not broken. Yara, the bitter leaf. The one for swelling. Chew it, and make a poultice, and bring it to me.”

Yara nodded, and she went to the plants they had been gathering, and her hands were shaking, just a little, and she made them steady, the way Amara had taught her, by breathing, slow, and by thinking about the work, and not about the fear, and she chewed the bitter leaf, and made the poultice, and brought it back, and Amara spread it on Elena’s ankle, and bound it with a strip of cloth from her satchel, neat and tight and kind.

“There,” Amara said, and she sat back on her heels, and wiped her hands on her skirt, and her voice was level, and carrying, the way it was when she was giving an order, or a warning, or both at once. “That will help the swelling. But you cannot walk on it, Elena Ruiz. Not today. Not alone. You will have to wait, and rest, and let it settle, and then someone will have to help you back to the path.”

“I cannot stay here,” Elena said, shaky and stubborn and brave all at once. “I have to get back. My team will worry. And the snares are still out there. Someone has to take them down. If I do not, who will?”

“We will,” Yara said, and her voice was steady, and it was her voice, and it was also, she realised, her mother’s voice, the way Oya’s voice sounded when she gave an order she wished she did not have to give. “We take them down. We have been taking them down. The wire, the snares, all of it. We do not leave them in our jungle.”

Elena looked at her, and her eyes were wide, and her face was pale, and she was quiet for a long moment, and Yara could see her thinking, and putting things together, the way Yara herself put things together when she read a trail: a girl with gold eyes, and faint blue markings, and a healer who knew herbs no human healer knew, and a village that did not exist on any map, and snares that kept disappearing, and a man with a camera who kept coming back to read his line.

“You take them down,” Elena said, slowly, and her voice was soft, and wondering, and a little bit afraid, all at once. “You are the ones taking them down. I have been finding empty gullies, and cut wire, and marks on trees that I could not read, and I thought —” she stopped, and laughed, a small, shaky laugh, “— I thought I was going mad. I thought the jungle was taking its own snares down. But it was you. It was you, all along.”

Yara felt her throat go tight, and her markings flare, bright and blue, before she could stop them, and she breathed them down again, slow and steady, and she looked at Amara, and Amara looked at her, and Amara nodded, once, very slightly, and Yara understood that the secret was already out, or half out, or out enough that pretending would only make it worse.

“We take them down,” Yara said, quietly, so that it was for Elena and not for the river, and not for the jungle. “Because they are in our home. And because they hurt animals that have done nothing wrong. And because the man who sets them will not stop, unless someone stops him. Do you understand me, Elena Ruiz?”

“I understand,” Elena said, and her voice was steady now, and her eyes were steady, and she reached out, and laid her hand over Yara’s hand, warm and human, and shaking, just a little, the way brave hands sometimes shake. “I understand. And I will keep your secret. I do not report people, Yara. I report animals. And I protect the jungle. That is what I do. If you are part of the jungle, then I protect you too.”

They stayed with Elena until the sun was high, and the river was bright, and her ankle had settled enough that she could stand, with help, and hobble, with help, and Yara and Amara walked her back to the human path, slowly, one step at a time, and Yara felt, with every step, the two worlds she lived between pulling at her, one in each hand, and she did not know which one to follow, and she followed both, because she did not know how to do anything else.

On the way, Elena talked, and Yara listened, and learned, the way she always did when someone talked about the human world, hungrily, and carefully, and with her whole body.

“I know the man who sets the snares,” Elena said, and her voice was low, and angry, and sad, all at once, and Yara recognised the anger, because it was the same anger she felt when she thought about the man in the trees. “I know him. His name is Dane. Dane Mercer. He is a trophy hunter. He has a camera, and a rifle, and a notebook, and he thinks he is the only person in this jungle who knows how to read a trail. He is wrong. He is very, very wrong. But he is patient, and he is greedy, and he will not stop until he has proof of whatever he thinks he has found.”

Yara went very still. Her whole body went still, the way a hunted animal goes still, and then, a heartbeat later, the way a hunter goes still, and she felt her markings flare, bright and blue, and she breathed them down again, slow and steady, and she said, very carefully, “Dane Mercer. That is his name?”

“That is his name,” Elena said, and she looked at Yara, and her eyes were serious, and sad, and she nodded, once, slow and certain. “And I hate what he does. I have hated it for years. He thinks the jungle is a shop, and the animals are things to buy and sell. He is not a hunter, Yara. A hunter respects what he hunts. Dane does not respect anything. He only wants.”

Yara nodded, her throat tight, thinking of the boot print, and the kneeling place, and the cough in the dark. Now the man had a name, and the name made him smaller, somehow, and bigger, both at once: smaller, because a man with a name is a man, and not a shadow, and bigger, because a man with a name is a man who will come back.

“Thank you,” Yara said, quietly, so that it was for Elena and not for the path, and not for the jungle. “Thank you for telling me his name. And thank you for hating what he does. That is —” she stopped, and smiled, a small, tired smile, “— that is more than most humans would do.”

At the human path, Elena stopped, and turned, and looked back at Yara, and at Amara, and her eyes were wide, and her face was pale, and she was quiet for a long moment, and Yara could see her thinking, and putting things together, and then she reached into her vest, and took out a small, worn notebook, and tore out a page, and held it out to Yara.

“Here,” Elena said, and her voice was soft, and steady, and brave, all at once. “This is for you. It is a sketch I made, of a footprint I found, by the river, two days ago. It is too big to be human. Too big, and too deep, and the toes are wrong. I did not know what it was. I still do not know what it was. But I think —” she stopped, and laughed, a small, shaky laugh, “— I think you might know. And I think, if you ever need a human who knows how Dane thinks, and how to stall him, and how to make a trail disappear on a map, then you know where to find me. I will be on the river. I will always be on the river.”

Yara took the page, and looked at it, and her breath caught, because the sketch was good, and true, and it was a Vey’kar footprint, unmistakably, big and deep and wrong-toed, and Elena had drawn it carefully, and kindly, and without fear, and she had given it to Yara, as a gift, and as a promise, both at once.

“I will keep it,” Yara said, and her voice was steady, and it was her voice, and it was also, she realised, her father’s voice, the way she remembered it from when she was seven: quiet, and curious, and brave in a quiet way. “And I will remember. Thank you, Elena Ruiz.”

Elena nodded, and turned, and hobbled away down the human path, slowly, one step at a time, and Yara watched her go, until she was gone, and the path was empty, and the jungle was quiet, and the river was bright, and the morning was ordinary again, except that it was not ordinary at all, and never would be again.

Zola was waiting for them at the edge of the clearing, when they came home, and she was not waiting patiently at all. She was pacing a short, worn track in the dust, three steps one way and three steps back, her spear tapping against her shoulder with every turn, and when she saw Yara she stopped dead, and her eyes went wide, and her markings flared, bright and blue, and her voice, when she spoke, was loud enough for the whole clearing to hear.

“Where have you been?” Zola said, and her voice was angry, and frightened, and relieved, all at once, and Yara recognised all three, because she felt all three herself. “You went to gather herbs, and you have been gone all morning, and nobody knew where you were, and I was beginning to think the man with the snares had taken you, and I was about to go after you, and I was about to —” she stopped, and her eyes went to the page in Yara’s hand, and to the sketch on it, and her voice went quieter, and colder, and more afraid. “What is that? Yara. What is that, and where did you get it?”

Yara took a breath, slow, and let it out, slow, and told her. She told her about the cry by the river, and the woman in the water, and the twisted ankle, and the poultice, and the snares, and the name, Dane Mercer, and the sketch, and the promise, and as she spoke she watched Zola’s face do something it almost never did: it went still, and then it went angry, and then it went afraid, and then it went still again, like a hand closing around a wound.

“A human,” Zola said, and her voice was flat, and cold, and it cut across the clearing like a blade. “You brought a human to our river, and you told her about the snares, and you told her about us, and you gave her our secret, and you let her walk away with a sketch of our footprint in her hand. Tell me, Yara. Is that leading, or is that hoping? Is that compassion, or is that the human in you, getting us all killed?”

The clearing went very slightly quiet, and Yara felt her temper flare, hot and sudden, the way it always did when someone insulted her mother, or her people, or her, and her markings flared with it, bright and blue, and she breathed, slow, and let the feeling be there without feeding it, the way Amara had taught her, and the way Jabari himself had taught her, on the training ground, when he had said to think, and not to burn.

“It is compassion,” Yara said, and her voice was level, and her hands were still at her sides, and she looked at Zola, and did not look away. “She was hurt, and alone, and she hates the snares, and she hates the man who sets them, and she knows his name, and she knows how he thinks, and she promised to keep our secret, and I believe her. I believe her, Zola, because I looked in her eyes, and I saw that she meant it. And if I am wrong, then I am wrong, and I will answer for it. But I would rather be wrong for helping, than right for walking past a hurt woman by a river.”

Zola looked at her for a long moment, her old friend’s eyes level and bright, and Yara made herself meet them, and not look away, and not dim her markings, and not shrink, and then, slowly, Zola’s shoulders dropped, and her spear lowered, and her face softened, and she laughed, very quietly, in the clearing, where everyone could hear it.

“You are a terrible leader,” Zola said, fond and teasing and relieved all at once, “who helps humans by rivers and believes their promises.”

“Tell her,” Yara said, laughing quietly, because it was true, and because it frightened her a little, and steadied her too. “Tell her I brought home a name, and a promise, and a friend, instead. Tell her that is what leading is.”

But Zola’s laugh faded, and her face went serious again, and she looked at Yara, and her eyes were afraid, and she said, quietly, so that only Yara could hear, “Yara. “I know,” Yara said, and her voice was steady, and her throat was tight, and she nodded, once, slow and certain. “I know he will. And I will answer him, when he does. That is what leading is, too.”

Oya was standing on the temple steps when Yara came to her, and her face was calm, and level, and afraid, all at once, and Yara understood, without being told, that Zola had already told her, or that Amara had already told her, or that a mother simply knows, the way mothers do, when her daughter has done something brave, and something dangerous, both at once.

“Tell me,” Oya said, and her voice was level, and carrying, and it settled over the clearing like a hand settling over a fire. “Tell me what happened by the river, Yara. All of it. Do not leave anything out. Not the ankle. Not the snares. Not the name. Not the sketch. All of it.”

Yara told her. She told her about the cry, and the woman in the water, and the twisted ankle, and the poultice, and the snares, and the name, Dane Mercer, and the sketch, and the promise, and as she spoke she watched her mother’s face, and she saw, in her mother’s face, the thing she had been afraid she would see: fear, and anger, and pride, all at once, and under them, slower and steadier, something she recognised now: love, and the weight of it, both at once.

Three days.
